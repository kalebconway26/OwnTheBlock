1. The verdict in one page

What's strong
  • A clear, emotional story (Ms. Jenkins, the eyesore next door, HBCU neighborhoods).
  • The Block Simulator: every number links to its formula, risk events show payouts dropping, 43 automated tests pass.
  • Honesty built in: on-chain vs off-chain labels, "estimates, not guarantees," downside cases.
  • A real legal path (Reg CF + one LLC per asset) instead of hand-waving.
  • The Charter concept: "the community decides, the code enforces."
What will hurt you
  • No blockchain is running yet. It's a crypto competition, and every transaction hash in the app is pretend.
  • The returns are overstated. With every real cost included, the spec's house earns about 3.5% a year, not 8.7%.
  • Rent-to-own math fails. It uses up the whole platform fee, and the tenant needs 88 years to reach a down payment.
  • The barbershop deal is about 20% APR. That's hard to square with "no predatory loans."
  • Legal conflicts: a smart-contract escrow, the Neighborhood Fund under Reg CF, and a peer-to-peer marketplace.
  • No outside validation: no real residents, owners or partners yet.

The one insight that fixes the economics

Don't buy houses at full price. Rescue distressed houses below their repaired value.

At full price, a $10 token is worth about $8.55 on day one after closing and offering costs. That leaves investors earning ~3.5% a year, less than a savings account.

Buy a boarded-up house for $90k, put $35k into repairs, and it's worth about $150k. Day-one value becomes ~$10.63 per $10 token, and the return rises to ~8.7% a year.

The social mission and the investment math point the same way: fixing eyesores is where the money is made. Make "Rescue the Eyesore" the core model, not a side feature.

The three changes to make before you present

  1. Put real transactions on Base Sepolia. Deploy at least the token, the raise, the payout contract and test USDC, and show one buy and one claim on the block explorer.
  2. Replace the spec's returns with the honest model (Section 8). Lead with the distressed-home case and show the full-price case as the reason you don't buy retail.
  3. Fix rent-to-own and the business terms (Sections 9 and 13). Use a lease-purchase option and a 1.25×–1.3× cap.

Phase 1. Find the property

  • Neighbors flag it in the app ("Flag an eyesore": photo + address), or stewards bring it (land bank, city vacant-property list, church members, estate attorneys).
  • OwnTheBlock staff pull public records: owner of record, tax status, liens, code violations, and probate status if the owner died.
  • Screening rule: the after-repair value (ARV) must exceed all-in cost by at least 10%. If it doesn't, the deal doesn't proceed.
  • Off-chain The flag and the community upvotes can be recorded in the app. Optionally, post a hash of the flag on-chain to timestamp it.

Phase 3. Form the LLC, write the Charter, sign the deal (weeks 6–10)

  1. Form "310 Demo Ct LLC", or a new series inside a pooled Block Fund LLC (recommended; see Section 13). OwnTheBlock Inc. is the manager. Investors are members. The steward holds a governance seat with a "golden share" veto over affordability rules.
  2. The operating agreement says the official list of members is kept by the transfer agent, which mirrors the on-chain token registry. 1 token = 1 membership unit.
  3. The neighborhood's Charter is attached: who can invest, caps, local floor, voting, rent cap, reserve, exit rules.
  4. Sign a purchase agreement with the seller, contingent on the raise succeeding. The seller agrees to wait 90–120 days to close. A small refundable deposit ($1–2k, paid from OwnTheBlock's operating funds) holds the deal.
  5. On-chain Deploy the asset's contracts: AssetToken, Raise, Treasury, Distributor, and RulesRegistry entries from the Charter.

Phase 4. File the offering and open the raise (weeks 10–20)

  1. The attorney prepares Form C: business plan, risks, use of funds, financials (CPA-reviewed if the raise is above about $124k; verify current thresholds), and the operating agreement. It's filed with the SEC.
  2. The offering goes live on the partner portal and is mirrored in the OwnTheBlock app. Reg CF requires it to be public for at least 21 days before any sale is final.
  3. Tier windows (or the Charter's own rules): days 1–7 zip-code residents, days 8–14 students and alumni, day 15+ any verified U.S. investor.
  4. Investors sign up (email or phone + passkey), pass KYC, take the 5-question risk quiz, and commit $10 or more. Money goes to the escrow bank. On-chain The Raise contract records each commitment (amount, tier, timestamp) with no personal data.
  5. Investors may cancel up to 48 hours before the deadline (Reg CF right). The app needs a Cancel button.
  6. Protections: the $2,500 yearly limit for most people (Reg CF; higher for higher incomes), a 24-hour cooling-off on a first investment over $100, and a check-in if someone invests more than 10% of their stated monthly income.

Phase 5. Close the raise and buy the house (weeks 20–24)

  1. Goal not met by the deadline: the escrow bank returns every dollar. The Raise contract marks all commitments refunded. On-chain
  2. Goal met: the escrow bank releases funds to the LLC's bank account. Tokens are minted to every investor's wallet in one transaction. On-chain The transfer agent records the same owner list. Off-chain
  3. Closing: North Carolina uses attorneys for closings. The attorney pays off liens and back taxes from the purchase price, the seller signs the deed, and the deed is recorded at the Guilford County Register of Deeds in the LLC's name. Off-chain
  4. The recorded deed, closing statement and insurance policy are uploaded. Their hashes are posted to the Money Trail. Both

Phase 6. Renovate in stages (months 6–9)

  • Repair money sits in the LLC's Treasury, either as USDC (multisig) or in a bank account with its balance mirrored.
  • Pay contractors in 3–4 draws: demolition/rough-in, systems, finishes, final. Each draw needs an invoice, inspection photos and two signatures (manager + steward). On-chain Treasury.spend(to, amount, memo) with the document hash.
  • Overruns: paid from the 15% contingency first. Beyond that, the community votes: pause, cut scope, or a small follow-on raise.
  • Hire local contractors first. This counts as an impact metric.

Phase 7. Lease up and collect rent (month 9 →)

  • The property manager markets the home, with first notice to neighborhood families and existing tenants on the block. Tenant screening follows fair-housing law: written criteria applied the same way to everyone. No investor or neighbor picks the tenant.
  • Offer the lease-purchase option (Section 13, idea 4) at move-in.
  • The tenant pays rent in dollars (ACH, card or cash at a partner location). Off-chain Rent is optionally reported to credit bureaus.
  • Late rent: a hardship fund and payment plan first, mediation second, eviction only as a last resort, with the steward informed. Publish this policy.

Phase 8. Pay investors every month (month 10 →)

  1. The PM sends a monthly report: rent collected, each expense, reserve added. It's uploaded, and its hash stored.
  2. Net cash is converted to USDC and deposited into the Distributor contract with the report hash. On-chain
  3. Each token holder's share becomes claimable. Holders tap Claim (gas paid by OwnTheBlock) or turn on auto-claim/auto-reinvest. They can cash out to a bank through Coinbase Offramp.

Phase 9. Govern, report, pay taxes (ongoing)

  • Proposals: repairs above the reserve limit, rent changes within the cap, sell/hold, refinance. Votes are on-chain through CommunityGovernor with the Charter's weights.
  • Charter changes need a 2/3 supermajority and a 30-day wait (timelock). On-chain
  • Annual: Form C-AR (Reg CF annual report), NC annual report, tax return. Investors get a K-1 (partnership), or a 1099-DIV if a REIT structure is used. The app exports earnings history.
  • Quarterly: a public transparency page with income statement, reserve balance, governance history and impact metrics.

Fixes to the spec's contract design

  • Raise: in production it doesn't hold investor dollars. It records commitments and mints at closing. On testnet it can hold MockUSDC to demonstrate the full flow, labeled "in production, a regulated escrow bank holds the funds."
  • Treasury: change "2 of 3 (manager, OwnTheBlock, steward)" to "steward signature always required." Or use 3-of-5 including 2 elected resident reps.
  • Admin powers: list every admin power in the app (pause, register KYC, deposit, migrate). Put them behind a multisig with a public log. Pause can never block refunds or claims (already in the spec).
  • Cancel commitment: add Raise.cancel() that works until 48 hours before the deadline.
  • Recovery and inheritance: the transfer agent can reissue tokens to a recovered or inherited wallet after identity checks. That's allowed because the tokens are permissioned, and it's a key safety feature.

6. How a property gets bought: six scenarios

In every case, the buyer is the LLC. People can't own part of a house directly, but they can own part of the LLC that owns it. Tokens are units of that LLC.

Scenario A: Paid-off house, owner wants to sell

1
Ms. Jenkins' neighbor (living) owns a house outright and wants to sell to the community instead of an investor.
2
Appraisal says $110k as-is. The purchase agreement is signed at $110k, contingent on the raise, with a 120-day close.
3
The raise covers price + closing + repairs + reserve + offering costs. At closing, the attorney pays any taxes owed, the seller gets the rest, and the deed goes to the LLC.

Timeline: 4–6 months. Simplest case. Lead with this one.

Scenario B: House with a mortgage

Same as A, except the attorney gets a payoff letter from the lender and pays the loan off at closing. The seller keeps the rest (their equity). If the seller owes more than the house is worth (a short sale), the lender must approve, which adds 2–4 months.

Scenario C: Bank-owned (foreclosure / REO)

The LLC makes an offer to the bank like any buyer. The problem: banks want fast cash closings, and a 90-day raise is slow. Fix: a bridge fund (first-loss partner or CDFI line of credit) buys quickly, then the raise refinances it within 6 months. Also ask about "first look" programs that give community buyers priority on foreclosed homes (verify local availability).

Scenario D: Heirs' property / eyesore (the Rescue the Eyesore case)

StructureFamily getsCommunity getsExample numbers
A. SellCash at appraised as-is value (minus back taxes and liens)The whole houseBuy $50k, repairs $35k + 15%, ARV $120k
B. Sell with buy-backCash now + right to buy back within 5 years at a preset priceRental income or a salePreset price = cost + 4%/yr

Hurdles: every heir must sign (often the slowest step, sometimes over a year), back taxes come out of the price, vacant-property insurance is expensive, and repairs can overrun.

Fixes to present: partner with a legal aid clinic for heirs' property, and don't open the raise until title is clear. Under Structure C, the family's large stake gets a Charter-approved exception to the per-person cap, and its votes are capped at 25% on community decisions.

The fairness question: "If the community buys at $50k and sells at $120k, did you take the family's equity?" Answer: the $70k gap is mostly the $35–40k of repairs plus risk. The family gets the full as-is value and can choose B or C to share in the upside. Show all three side by side.

Scenario E: Land bank, city or tax-foreclosed property

Cities and counties often sell vacant or tax-foreclosed lots and houses at a discount to buyers who promise to fix and occupy them. The steward (city or CDC) brings the property in, gets a governance seat, and puts affordability in the deed. This is the best pipeline for scale. Ask the City of Greensboro what vacant-property programs it has.

Scenario F: House with a tenant already living there

The LLC buys with the tenant in place, so the lease carries over. The tenant gets the lease-purchase option on day one and isn't displaced. That's a strong anti-gentrification message.

What about borrowing (leverage)?

The spec says no leverage for users, which is right. At the property level, a loan covering 50–60% of the price (for example from a CDFI) would cut the raise in half and raise investor yield. It also adds the risk of losing the house in a bad year. Recommendation: no property debt in the pilot. Consider a modest CDFI loan once there's an operating track record.

8. The money: how profit is made and how long ROI takes

Where investors' returns come from

  1. Monthly rent income: what's left after vacancy, management, reserve, taxes, insurance, admin and fees.
  2. Appreciation: the house is worth more when sold, through the market rising or, mainly, the repairs adding value.
  3. Business repayments: a share of revenue until the cap (Section 9).

Return of your original $10 mostly comes at the exit. Rent alone takes 15–28 years to pay back $10, so the exit plan is essential.

Investor returns are before business failures. If about 1 in 10 deals fails partway, expected returns drop by a few points. The first-loss partner absorbs part of that.

Right comparison: merchant cash advances, not SBA loans

Businesses turned down by banks often take merchant cash advances, which commonly cost far more than 40% APR (verify with a source for your pitch). Against those, 12% with no collateral and payments that shrink in slow months is a strong offer. Against an SBA microloan (often around 8–13%), it's comparable but needs no personal guarantee or collateral. Make that the pitch.

Revenue verification (the cash-business problem)

  • Require a connected card terminal (Square, Stripe or Clover) as a condition of funding. Revenue flows in automatically.
  • For cash sales, require monthly bank deposit statements. Investors get a defined percentage of bank deposits, not self-reported numbers.
  • Allow a payment pause of up to 2 months a year (counted against the cap), with a vote.
  • Pair each deal with free bookkeeping help from A&T accounting students, which also counts as impact.

13. Brainstorm: ways to make it better

Ranked by how much each idea strengthens the pitch for the effort.

1. One Block Fund LLC that owns homes directly high impact

Instead of one LLC per house plus a fund that holds their tokens, form one fund LLC per neighborhood that owns every house directly. Investors can still "back" a specific house through an earmark, or each house can be a series.

Solves: the investment company problem, the K-1 cost (one form per investor), admin costs ($250 → $80 a month per house) and single-house risk.

Trade-off: it feels less like "I own 1204 Sample St." Fix that with the earmark and photos.

2. Make Rescue the Eyesore the main model high impact

Buying below repaired value is what makes the numbers work: ~8.7% instead of ~3.5%. It's also the best story: the boarded-up house next to Ms. Jenkins. Rename the pitch around it, e.g. "Fix the block, own the block."

3. Real testnet contracts with a judge-proof demo high impact

Coinbase Smart Wallet (passkey) + paymaster + MockUSDC + Raise + Distributor on Base Sepolia. Let a judge scan a QR code, get test dollars, buy 1 token and see it on the explorer in under 60 seconds.

4. Lease-purchase instead of 2% tokens high impact

  • At move-in, the tenant gets an option to buy at today's appraisal + 3% a year, exercisable in years 3–7.
  • $100 of each month's rent goes into a down-payment credit (counted as an operating cost: it lowers investor yield by about 0.8 points).
  • Stack it with NC Housing Finance Agency down-payment assistance and rent reporting to credit bureaus.
  • Why it's better: a real path to ownership in 3–5 years (not 88), and the tenant buying out the investors gives investors a planned exit.

5. Rent history as a portable credential medium-high

Record each on-time payment as an attestation on Base through the Ethereum Attestation Service (EAS). The tenant owns a verifiable rent record they can show any lender. It's a blockchain use a database can't easily copy, and it fits "build credit."

6. First-loss capital from banks, foundations and HBCUs medium-high

Banks must invest in low- and moderate-income communities under the Community Reinvestment Act. A bank putting $50k of first-loss capital behind resident investors is a clean, believable story. Also: foundation impact investments, city housing funds, HBCU endowment impact allocations.

7. Software licensing to land trusts, CDCs and cities medium-high

The business model fix. These groups already own properties but can't take $10 investments from residents. OwnTheBlock becomes their engine, revenue doesn't come from residents, and it scales through partners.

8. $10-a-month auto-invest and round-ups medium

Turns $0.68 into a habit. Show the 5-year projection for $10, $25 and $40 a month in the app.

9. Validation you can put on a slide medium

  • A 10-question survey of 50+ residents and students: would you invest $10? What worries you?
  • One letter of interest from a homeowner or heir family and one from a business owner.
  • A meeting request to a local neighborhood group, an A&T real estate or business program, or the city's housing department.

10. Permanent affordability through a land trust partnership medium

A community land trust owns the land and investors own the building. Resale prices are limited, so homes stay affordable forever. It's a strong answer to "aren't you just speeding up gentrification?"
